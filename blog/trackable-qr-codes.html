Why I Switched From Static to Trackable QR Codes

By Ali · QR Studio Blog

The first QR codes I ever made were "static" — meaning once I generated them, that was it. They worked fine for scanning, but they were completely silent about everything else. I had no idea if anyone was actually scanning them, how many people, or when. I only found out these numbers even existed after I printed 200 flyers with a QR code on them and genuinely had no way to know if the code was working, being ignored, or even scanned once.

What "trackable" actually means

A trackable (or "dynamic") QR code doesn't encode your final link directly. Instead, it points to a small redirect step first — one that quietly logs that a scan happened, then immediately sends the person on to your real link. To the person scanning, it feels identical: point camera, get redirected, done in under a second. But now you have a record of it.

What it actually showed me

Once I switched, a few things became obvious that I genuinely didn't expect:

  • Scans were heavily concentrated in the evening, not the afternoon — something I couldn't have guessed.
  • One flyer location I thought was working well had almost zero scans, while a spot I almost skipped was doing most of the work.
  • I could tell within the first day whether a new batch of prints was even worth the cost, instead of waiting weeks to guess from sales numbers.

The other advantage: you can edit it after printing

This is the part that actually sold me. With a static code, if the link changes — say your website moves, or a promotion ends — the printed QR code is now permanently wrong, and you have to reprint everything. With a trackable one, the QR code image never changes; only the destination behind it does. I've updated the same printed code to point to three different pages over time without touching the original print.

When a static code is still fine

To be fair, not everything needs tracking. A WiFi password QR code, or one linking to something permanent and personal, works perfectly well as a plain static code — no need to overcomplicate it. Tracking earns its place specifically when you're trying to measure something: a campaign, a flyer, an ad placement, different locations.
